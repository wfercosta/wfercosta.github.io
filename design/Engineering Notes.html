Designing process platforms for long-running business workflows

Notes from a year rebuilding a proposal-to-contract platform: why state machines outlived microservices, how idempotency keys became the real architecture, and the tradeoffs nobody writes about.

Wander Costa
Software engineer · Brazil
cover · process platform diagram

There is a class of software that looks boring from the outside and is genuinely hard on the inside. Mortgage proposals. Underwriting. Insurance claims. The things that take weeks, involve a dozen human approvals, and have to remember exactly where they were when the power went out.

I spent most of last year rebuilding one of these. Here's what held up and what didn't.

State machines, not microservices

The first version was a pile of microservices talking to each other via REST and a queue. It worked, in the way that a Rube Goldberg machine works — technically correct, practically unbearable. Every new requirement meant editing four services and praying.

The second version started with the state machine. We drew the proposal lifecycle on a whiteboard — draft → submitted → analysed → approved → contracted → active → closed — and made that the primary artifact. Services became workers that picked up transitions; the state itself lived in one place.

"The architecture isn't the boxes and lines. It's the thing you can point at and say 'this is the source of truth'."

Why this matters

Once the state is explicit, everything else becomes easier: reporting, resuming after a crash, explaining the system to new hires, writing contracts with the business side. The moment your business lives in ad-hoc fields scattered across tables, you've lost.

Idempotency keys are the real architecture

If I had to pick one thing that saved us more than any pattern or framework, it would be idempotency keys. Every single write — every one — carries a key derived from (actor, intent, target, version). The platform is allowed to retry anything it wants, and nothing happens twice.

def submit_proposal(proposal_id, actor):
    key = idempotency_key("submit", actor, proposal_id, version=1)
    with store.idempotent(key):
        proposal = repo.get(proposal_id)
        proposal.submit(at=now())
        repo.save(proposal)
        events.emit(ProposalSubmitted(proposal_id))

This sounds boring. It is boring. It's also the difference between a platform you can operate and one you can't.

The tradeoffs nobody writes about

  • Debuggability over elegance. We chose verbose, explicit code over clever patterns every time someone would be on-call at 2am reading it.
  • Slow by default. Long-running workflows don't need to be fast. They need to be correct and resumable. Optimising for latency before either of those is how you ship bugs.
  • Human in the loop. Every workflow assumed a human could intervene. Not as a bolt-on — designed in from the start.

If you're building something in this space and want to compare notes, I'm reachable here.

Hi, I'm Wander

Software engineer based in Brazil. I write about systems that outlive their first whiteboard sketch.

I've been building software for about fifteen years. Most of it in the boring, load-bearing parts of industries that can't afford to be boring — mortgage, banking, healthcare, logistics. The kind of systems where a rounding error is a legal problem and "we'll fix it in the next sprint" isn't a plan.

My day job is platform engineering and architecture. I care a lot about the seams between teams, the interfaces between services, and the quiet decisions that determine whether a codebase stays pleasant to work in after year three.

This site is where I write about that. It's also where I keep notes on things I'm learning — Go, Kotlin, agentic systems, data platforms, the occasional detour into K8s internals. I don't publish on a schedule. I publish when I have something that's been rattling around long enough to be worth the effort of writing it down.
